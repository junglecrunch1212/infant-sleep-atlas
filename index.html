    Infant sleep doctrine, scored by the odds it’s true

    For a healthy four-month-old who already sleeps eleven hours with one feed: the safety rules are near-certain, claims about this month’s sleep mechanics are mostly coin-flips, and claims that any practice inside normal loving care changes her long-term development are almost all unlikely.

    Every claim here carries a probability: the chance that it would hold up in an adequately powered, unbiased study. Direct evidence is weighted by size, design and measurement, then checked against causal mechanism and the adjacent literature; contrary evidence counts by its own quality. A small, unreplicated study that fits the mechanism earns a real probability. Absence of evidence moves a claim toward its mechanistic prior, not to zero. Two scorers worked independently and their median gap was 0.05.

    The ten popular claims about long-term outcomes have a median probability of 0.13; the highest, at 0.50, is the attachment camp’s claim that responsive night care builds security. The twenty-five claims about short-term sleep mechanics have a median of 0.30. Commercial claims score lowest (median 0.22), with the contrarian camp’s harm claims (0.15). Claims carried by consensus momentum are coin-flips (0.51), not errors. The evidence-based safety rules score 0.84 to 0.97. Plate III scores all 54; Plate I lists what to change this week.

    Plate II

    Sleep explains under 1% of later outcomes; waking-hours interaction explains 6–27%

    The question "which sleep practice is best for her long-term development" has a scale problem before it has an answer. Measured In school-age children, sleep duration correlates r = .08 with cognitive performance and r = .09 with behaviour problems across 86 studies and 35,936 children, and not at all with intelligence (Astill 2012); with objectively measured sleep the figure is r = .06 (Short 2018). That is under 1% of variance, observational, before confounding is removed.

    For infancy specifically, the best systematic review (22 studies of sleep at 0–18 months against Bayley, WPPSI and similar tests) found 53.5% of about 101 tested associations null, 39.6% mixed and 5.9% consistently significant, two of those six in the wrong direction. Study power ranged from 0.06 to 0.88; one study reached 0.80 (Butler 2024). Sleeping through at 6 or 12 months had no association with Bayley scores at 6, 12 or 36 months in 388 infants (Pennestri 2018); three or more wakings a night at 8 months made no Bayley-III difference at 8 or 24 months (Mäkelä 2018).

    The positive signals that do exist share a signature. Outcomes measured by direct testing are mostly unrelated to infant sleep. Outcomes rated by the same parent who rated the sleep show small associations: 0.14–0.18 SD higher mother-rated internalising after sleep problems at 6 months in 86,724 Norwegian infants (Valla 2021). Teacher-rated outcomes shrink toward zero: effect size −0.12 for infant-limited sleep problems at age 10–11 in 5,107 Australian children, with no difference in school achievement (Williamson 2020). That is what shared-reporter variance plus modest confounding produces.

    The associations also sit in the tails. The shortest-sleeping 18% across 3–24 months scored 5.2 Bayley points, about 0.35 SD, lower at age 2 (Smithson 2018); combined sleep, crying and feeding problems carried d = 0.41 for later behaviour problems across 22 studies (Hemmi 2011). She is in none of those groups. Several widely quoted results are exposure-window mismatches: the Quebec short-sleep finding used sleep from age 2.5 to 6 (Touchette 2007), the Norwegian result is from 18 months (Sivertsen 2015), and the bedtime-regularity findings are from ages 3–7 (Kelly 2013).

    Two designs test causation, and neither supports a large effect. The only genetically informed analysis attributes the link between day/night sleep at 6 months and later language mainly to shared genes (1,029 twins, Dionne 2011). Randomised trials that changed sleep parenting produced no child difference in behaviour, sleep, stress or relationship measures at ages 2 and 6 (Hiscock 2008; Price 2012); BMI reductions in two trials faded by ages 9–11 (Paul 2025; Taylor 2025). No trial has measured cognition or language after an infant sleep intervention.

    Modelled Replacing the entire rearing family raised IQ at 18–20 by 4.4 points in a Swedish sibling design (Kendler 2015), and mean heritability across traits is 49% (Polderman 2015). One practice difference inside an already favourable home (45- versus 60-minute naps, rocked versus self-settled onset, one night feed versus none) therefore plausibly moves long-term cognition by well under one IQ point. That bound is an inference from behaviour genetics, not a measurement.

    What the nulls can and cannot exclude

    The main "no long-term harm" sleep-training study followed 225 of 326 children (69%) to age 6 and found an SDQ difference of +0.5 points (95% CI −1.0 to +1.9, on an SD near 5.8). That excludes intention-to-treat harm above roughly 0.33 SD. But only 100 of 174 intervention mothers attended a sleep consultation, families chose their own mix of methods, and no infant under 7 months was enrolled (Price 2012). Modelled Harm confined to infants who actually underwent controlled crying could reach 0.65–0.7 SD and go undetected.

    The attachment trial had about 10 dyads per arm at 12 months, roughly 15% power for a 20-point difference in security (Gradisar 2016). The cohort study of leaving infants to cry had 80% power only for a 20-point difference and measured one coarse "ever left to cry" item (Bilgin & Wolke 2021). The bed-sharing nulls are better powered: no association between bed-sharing at 9 months and internalising or externalising trajectories from 3 to 11 years in 16,599 UK children (Bilgin 2024).

    Large effects in either direction are ruled out for the practices studied; small effects (0.1–0.3 SD) are not; no sleep-training trial has followed children past age 6. Infants high in negative emotionality are more affected by both poor and good parenting across 84 longitudinal studies (Slagt 2016), which by inference makes the choice among reasonable practices lower-stakes for an easy infant. That inference is untested for sleep, and temperament at 4 months predicts later temperament only weakly (Kagan 1998).

    Plate III

    Fifty-four claims, scored by the odds they hold up

    How each number was set

    • P is a probability, not a verdict. It is the chance that the claim, as worded on its card, would be confirmed by an adequately powered, unbiased study in healthy term infants.
    • Direct evidence is weighted by size, design (trial over cohort over survey), measurement (video or actigraphy over parent report) and replication.
    • Mechanism and adjacent literature set the prior: does the claim follow from known physiology or learning, and does it hold in older children, adults or animals?
    • Small studies count. A small, unreplicated result that fits the mechanism and its neighbours earns a real probability. Missing evidence pulls a claim toward its mechanistic prior, not to zero.
    • Contrary evidence pulls P down by its own quality: an objective trial outweighs a parent survey.
    • The range on each card is the scorer’s own uncertainty about the number.

    Your hypothesis 1 Popular advice is shaped by what time-poor, dual-income parents want to hear. Holds for what the advice promises the child; fails for what it promises parents
    • The ten claims that mainly serve time-poor parents have a median P of 0.30: sleeping through matters (0.09), top-ups lengthen sleep (0.12), teething explains bad nights (0.30), night-weaning helps the baby (0.30), her own room improves her sleep (0.38).
    • But the convenience itself is real: sleep training cuts signalled waking and improves parental sleep (P 0.85). What fails is the leap from “the parents sleep better” to “the baby sleeps more” (0.30) or “the baby turns out better” (0.10).
    • Official safety advice is the opposite of convenient, and parental stress did not predict use of cry-it-out methods among 586 parents; beliefs about dependence did (Maute & Perren 2018).
    Your hypothesis 2 A 30-year scientific consensus has crowded out dissent and may be overturned. Momentum claims are coin-flips held with too much confidence; no evidence of suppression
    • The six claims carried by consensus momentum have a median P of 0.51: drowsy but awake (0.53), sleep associations (0.59), contact-nap habits (0.53), room-sharing to 12 months (0.48). Stated as settled, they are closer to even odds; the overreach is the confidence, not the direction.
    • Precedent is real: prone sleeping was recommended until 1988 although pooled data showed harm by 1970 (Gilbert 2005), and 40.2% of 363 tested medical practices were reversed (Prasad 2013).
    • Against suppression: dissent is published in mainstream journals, has changed guidance in England, Norway and Spain, and cut the AAP’s room-sharing advice from a year to six months.
    The contrarian counter-claim The attachment and “natural” camp is the better guide. Its harm claims score as low as the industry’s benefit claims
    • Median P 0.15 across its four claims: sleep training causes lasting harm (0.14), bed-sharing benefits development (0.15), contact naps benefit development (0.16).
    • Its strongest claim, that responsive night care feeds attachment, scores 0.50: the general sensitivity–attachment link is causal, but promptness is not sensitivity.
    • Commercially, the two camps mirror each other: the 22 claims that serve sleep products, programmes or consultants have a median of 0.22.
    The null Within normal loving care, sleep-practice choices barely reach long-term outcomes. Best supported: outcome claims have a median P of 0.13
    • Every multi-year follow-up is null or trivial: sleep training to 6 years, cry-out and attachment, bed-sharing to 11 years (n = 16,599) and to 18, room-sharing to 8, sleeping through versus development.
    • The nulls exclude large effects, not small ones, which is why no outcome claim scores below 0.05 (Plate II).

    Plate IV

    Her residual risk is a few per 100,000, concentrated in avoidable scenarios

    Measured US sudden unexpected infant death (SIDS, plus unknown cause, plus accidental suffocation and strangulation in bed) ran at 93.6 per 100,000 live births in 2024 and has not improved since about 2000 (CDC). In the CDC case registry, at least 76% of deaths involved multiple unsafe sleep factors and 0.9% had none documented (Erck Lambert 2024).

    The cause mix shifts with age. Among 250 explained suffocations, overlay had a median age of 2 months with none after 7 months; soft-bedding deaths a median of 3 months; wedging a median of 6 months, most often between mattress and wall or mattress and headboard. In infants aged 5–11 months, blankets obstructed the airway in 55% of soft-bedding deaths (Erck Lambert 2019). A comparison of younger and older victims concludes that "rolling into objects in the sleep area is the predominant risk factor for older infants" (Colvin 2014). That is her age band and her new skill.

    Four caveats bound the bed-sharing row. The two estimates for older infants rest on few exposed cases (Blair's on one), though a third analysis also found none (1.07, Tappin 2005), and US surface-sharing carries an adjusted OR of 2.1–2.5 without adjustment for alcohol or drugs (Parks 2023). All hazard-stratified data pre-date 2007. SIDS case-control studies under-capture accidental suffocation and entrapment, which is exactly the mechanism that remains in adult beds at 5–8 months; unadjusted 1990s product-safety data put suffocation risk 20–40 times higher in adult beds than in cribs, with no adjustment for alcohol, smoking or sofas (Scheers 2003). And no study has examined bed-sharing by mothers who feed pumped milk by bottle: the protective behaviours seen on video (infant at breast height below the pillows, mother curled around her, synchronised arousals) were observed in mothers nursing at the breast, while 71% of formula-fed infants lay at face height on or between pillows (Bartick 2022; Ball 2006).

    Bed-sharing, applied to this family

    At about 4 months she is past the 15-week window in which Carpenter finds significant hazard-free risk, though not by a wide margin. Here the US absolute and the UK risk-stratified position diverge, and the UK position fits the hazard-stratified data better. Finishing the night in the parents' bed is the pattern English investigators coded as "taken to and found in parental bed": elevated before adjustment, not significant after adjusting for alcohol, heavy duvets, parental tiredness and crowding, and not significant beyond 14 weeks or for non-smokers (Blair 1999). The exposure that matters is the unplanned 4 am transfer, made when tiredness peaks, into a bed nobody prepared. Room-sharers have four times the odds of exactly that (Paul 2017).

    Scored the same way as every other claim: P 0.38 that a prepared bed, with a sober, non-smoking, breastfeeding mother, raises her all-cause sudden-death risk at least 1.5-fold over a crib in your room. If it does, the absolute addition is a few per 100,000. P 0.93 that bed-sharing after alcohol or sedatives sharply raises it.

    Judgement Decide each evening. Either the bed is set up and both adults are sober and rested, in which case bringing her in after the night feed is a defensible choice on current evidence, or it is not, in which case she goes back to the crib without exception. The mother returns her to the crib if she herself feels unusually depleted. A mother who nurses at the breast at night and has bed-shared before plausibly keeps the protective behaviours; the parent who gives a bottle should not be the one beside her.

    Plate V

    The bassinet standard ends at rolling or hands-and-knees, so move her now

    The federal bassinet rule incorporates ASTM F2194, whose scope is sleep "for an infant up to approximately 5 months in age or when the child begins to push up on hands and knees, whichever comes first", with a required fall-hazard warning at that point or at the maker's weight limit. Weight limits are typically 15–20 lb. Some manufacturers set the stop earlier: Chicco says to stop once the baby can roll over or push up "even if they have not yet reached the maximum weight limit", and an Evenflo bassinet insert says stop when the baby can "push up on hands and knees or can roll over".

    The mechanism is physical. Shallow sides and a high centre of gravity make falls and tip-overs possible once she pushes up, and a small soft-sided space lets a rolled infant end up face-against-side or in a corner, worsened by any tilt. CPSC logged more than 200 bassinet incidents, including 7 deaths, from 2017 to 2022 (Consumer Reports 2024). Limits of this evidence: no count of deaths specific to rolling in an over-age bassinet was found, no study compares bassinets with cribs by age, and the wording of the 2025 standard update could not be verified.

    Judgement P 0.73 that staying in the bassinet past rolling raises her risk: no death series exists for over-stage use, but the mechanisms are concrete and the cost of acting is a crib. The most permissive reading of her model's label buys a few weeks at most. Read the label tonight; if it names rolling, she is already past it.

    The compliant replacements that keep her in your room are a full-size crib (16 CFR 1219), a mini crib (16 CFR 1220) or a play yard (16 CFR 1221), each with the manufacturer's flat mattress or pad, a fitted sheet and nothing else. Mini cribs and play yards are rated until the child reaches 35 inches or can climb out, which covers the whole first year (ASTM F406). A bedside sleeper does not solve the problem, because its standard carries the same 5-month, hands-and-knees limit (ASTM F2906); neither does a SNOO, in-bed sleeper, pod or nest.

    Plate VII

    One written protocol, the same for every caregiver

    Sitting-device deaths were 2.8 times as likely (95% CI 1.5–5.2) under a child-care provider's supervision, and 2.0 times under a babysitter's, as under a parent's (Liaw 2019). A new or unusual sleep location was 6.5 times as common in sofa deaths (Rechtman 2014). A hired caregiver holding a sleeping infant for two to three hours a day in a comfortable chair is a foreseeable drowsiness scenario. Hand this card over, and run it yourselves.

    Sleep rules for anyone caring for her

    Every sleep, every caregiver, no exceptions.

    • Every sleep starts on her back in the crib or play yard, in an arms-free sleep sack, with nothing else in it. No swaddle. Nothing weighted.
    • Contact naps only while you are fully alert, sitting upright, with her face visible. At the first heaviness of your eyelids, put her down on her back or hand her to a parent. Saying so is expected and never penalised.
    • Never a contact nap lying back on a sofa or recliner.
    • No alcohol, cannabis or sedating medication while caring for her.
    • In a carrier: face visible, chin off her chest, close enough to kiss. No hands-free feeding.
    • Asleep in the car seat or stroller? Move her to the crib when you arrive. Never leave her sleeping in a car seat indoors, a swing, on a nursing pillow or lounger, or on an adult bed.
    • If she rolls onto her front by herself, leave her there. Always start her on her back.
    • Same bedtime routine, same steps, same order as the parents.
    • Nap ends at about 45 minutes? If she wakes content, the nap is over. If she wakes upset, rock her on.
    • Stirring, grunting or a brief call: wait 2–4 minutes. Real crying: go at once.

    Plate VIII

    Change little, because nothing is broken

    Her pattern (an 11-hour night with one feed and immediate resettling, 45-minute contact naps) is at or above the reference mean on every available measure, and every sleep-intervention efficacy trial enrolled infants with a parent-reported problem she does not have.     Night locationRoom-share to ~6 months; preference after

    Keep her in your room on her own surface to about 6 months: the old case-control data are consistent, the mechanism (lighter sleep and faster adult response near parents) is plausible, and a quarter of sudden deaths occur at 4–6 months. From 6 to 12 months no age-specific data exist, but the mechanism plausibly persists: P 0.48 that room-sharing still lowers risk then. Modelled If her residual risk over months 6–12 is 1–3 per 100,000, even a true halving from room-sharing is worth about 1 per 100,000. The measured cost of room-sharing falls on maternal sleep, not infant sleep (Volkovich 2015), and more weeks of room-sharing in months 0–6 showed no relation to sleep or behaviour problems at ages 6–8 in 193 Dutch children (Beijers 2019). After 6 months, stay if it costs you nothing.

    Night feedKeep it

    One feed at 4 months is normal; about 60% of 6–12-month-olds still take one (Brown & Harries 2015), and night weaning was not associated with longer night sleep in 193 infants followed to 52 weeks (Olson 2026). Feed in near-darkness, under dim warm light, with minimal interaction; nursing directly delivers time-matched milk automatically. Before lifting her, confirm she is awake and asking: infants vocalise in active sleep, and in a London video cohort a short pause before feeding in the early weeks was the only "limit-setting" component that predicted long night sleep at 3 months (St James-Roberts 2017).

    Sleep onsetKeep feeding and rocking; two free hedges

    Feeding or rocking her fully to sleep is unlikely to cost her anything lasting (P 0.08). It probably does raise signalled waking at 6–12 months (P 0.59), a cost that falls mostly on whoever answers. Blind-coded bedtime video found specific maternal settling behaviours unrelated to infant sleep disruption once age was controlled, while the caregiver's emotional availability was related (Teti 2010). The trials that taught the alternative gained 22–35 parent-reported minutes that were gone by 12 months, which fits a small real effect.

    Judgement Two zero-cost hedges, each a little better than even odds (P 0.53 for the drowsy put-down). Move the feed to the start of the routine and finish with rocking, which mirrors the one observational pointer that low bedtime nursing goes with faster consolidation (Philbrook & Teti 2016). And a few times a week, when she is calm, put her down drowsy; if she protests beyond mild fussing, pick her up and finish as usual. Nothing in the evidence says persisting through distress benefits her, and she is below the age at which any professional body endorses extinction methods.

    NapsContact naps fine; stop extending by default

    Contact naps can continue as long as the awake-holder rule can be honoured. P 0.16 that they benefit her development, P 0.53 that they make independent naps harder to establish later; the cost if so is a few weeks of transition. Stop extending the 45-minute nap by default. A 45-minute nap is one sleep cycle (infant cycles run 50–60 minutes, range 30–70, Grigg-Damberger 2016); the nap-and-memory experiments got their effects with naps averaging 45 minutes (Friedrich 2015); nap duration did not predict retention (Seehagen 2015); her 24-hour total is already mid-range; and unassisted lengthening is expected from 5–6 months. P 0.12 that extending short naps helps. Her state is the only readout: if she wakes content, the nap is over; if she wakes crying or stays irritable through the next wake period, rock her on.

    Twin data make daytime sleep the most environment-shaped part of infant sleep (shared environment 57–61% at 15–18 months, Fisher 2012; Brescianini 2011), so naps will take whatever shape the household gives them. Judgement If you want optionality, one low-stakes cot put-down a day at the first nap, when sleep pressure is highest, preserves it; before 6 months that nap happens in a room with an awake adult. For transfers, the one physiological study (about 21 crying infants, per press summaries; authors hold a related patent application) found 5 minutes of steady walking induced sleep, infants laid down before about 8 minutes of sleep often woke, and the arousal trigger was detachment from the adult's body rather than contact with the mattress (Ohmura 2022): walk, then sit still 5–8 minutes, then separate slowly. One small study, but it fits the transport calming response shown in both human infants and mouse pups (Esposito 2013): P 0.65 that it replicates.

    Pumped milkLabel by clock time; match the bin

    Milk composition cycles: melatonin peaks near 47 pg/mL around midnight and is undetectable by day; cortisol peaks in the early morning (83 reports, Italianer 2020). Infant-outcome evidence is one anonymous survey of 329 mothers linking mistimed milk to later sleep onset (Booker 2022) and one null (Toorop 2020).     Light, timing, routineHold the wake time; bright days, dim evenings

    Hold the 7:30 am wake time: wake time is the fixed end of infant sleep, and timing is the one sleep dimension that tracks across the first year (rank correlation 0.55 from 3 to 12 months, against 0.24 for night sleep duration, Schoch 2020). Leave bedtime at 8:30 pm; national mean bedtimes span 19:27 to 22:17 (Mindell 2010). Give her bright daylight in wake periods, outdoors in the early afternoon where possible; young infants spent only one-eighth of daytime hours above 100 lux in the one home study (Tsai 2012). Keep the last hour before bed and all night feeds dim and warm-toned (P 0.60 that this helps her fall asleep; no lux threshold has been validated in infants). Daylight by day: P 0.63. A dark room at night (P 0.45) and blackout for naps (P 0.40) are coin-flips at zero cost; summer dawn light is the likeliest trigger for early waking, so blackout at night is worth having. The routine is 20–30 minutes, same steps in the same order, ending in the sleep space, run identically by parents and nanny.

    Iron, pacifier, sound, trackingMeasure, don't guess

    Measure ferritin and haemoglobin now. If she is replete, skip drops and start iron-rich foods (meat or fortified foods) at about 6 months; if she is low, supplement. In one trial, infants with haemoglobin under 110 g/L benefited from iron while replete infants given iron grew less (Dewey 2002), and 1 mg/kg/day from 4 to 9 months in 221 non-anaemic breastfed infants changed no Bayley score to 36 months (Svensson 2024). Routine drops help an iron-replete infant: P 0.14. Vitamin D above the standard 400 IU does not improve sleep (Seppälä 2026).

    A pacifier at sleep onset is optional and need not be replaced when it falls out; from 6 months, limiting it to sleep times cut ear infections by 29% in one cluster trial (Niemelä 2000). If she finds her fingers, leave her to it. White noise: P 0.48, likeliest to help daytime naps in a noisy house. A sound machine, if used, sits across the room and reads no more than about 50 dBA at the crib (all 14 machines tested exceeded 50 dBA at 30 cm at full volume, Hugh 2014). Track sleep only for a week or two when testing a specific change: night-to-night variation within one infant is 40–45 minutes (SD) at this age, and continuous tracking converts that noise into false signals. A video monitor is useful because it shows unsignalled waking and resettling; an oxygen or heart-rate wearable is not.

    Plate IX

    What changes between now and her first birthday

    Rank-order stability of infant sleep is weak beyond a few months: night sleep at 3 months correlates 0.53 with 6 months and 0.24 with 12 months (Schoch 2020). A good 4-month sleeper is likely to stay in the low-problem majority: in one birth cohort, 6% of infants without an early sleep problem had one in the third year, against 21% of those with one (Byars 2012). Modelled Prevalence shifts between 3 and 9 months imply that between one in five and one in three settled infants has a noticeable stretch of more waking in the second half-year; no cohort reports that fraction directly. Lasting relapse after 6 months was rare in the one cohort that tracked it (1 of 29, Henderson 2020).

    Plate XI

    The one risk of unlimited attention: answering sounds that aren't requests

    Video and actigraphy agree that essentially all infants wake two to four times a night throughout the first year; 78–90% of those awakenings are vocalised, and the commonest single pattern is "vocalised, no intervention" (Goodlin-Jones 2001). What separates "good" from "poor" sleepers is whether a parent arrives.

    Four observational findings point the same way. In 107 families filmed at 1 and 3 months, more responses to infant distress went with a steeper decline in night waking to 9 months, while in solitary sleepers more interventions when the infant was not distressed, or was asleep, went with a slower decline (Voltaire & Teti 2018). In 80 infants filmed across the year, longer parental response time at 3 months (mean 3.3 minutes) was one of three predictors of unaided resettling at 12 months (Burnham 2002). Bedtime involvement at 4 months predicted rising night wakings to 8 months in 207 families (Matzliach 2025). In camera data from 3,158 infants, each additional parental crib visit raised the odds of the parent perceiving a sleep problem by 18% (Nanit-funded, cross-sectional, Kahn 2025).

    Scored: P 0.70 that the pause increases unaided resettling. All of it is observational, part of it runs backwards (poorly sleeping infants draw parents in, Simard 2008), and the one trial that advised waiting before attending found no objective effect (Santos 2019). But four independent studies agree, a responsive-parenting trial that taught “give a few minutes” gained 40 reported minutes (Lavner 2023), and operant learning in young infants is well established. The outcome at stake is signalled waking, the parents' experience, with no demonstrated bearing on her long-term development. Impose the pause deliberately for stirring, grunting and brief calls, and respond without delay to real crying. This is not extinction, and no source shows it harms.

    The same error has other forms in a high-resource household, each buying nothing measurable: rocking through every 45-minute arousal by default; continuous tracking and physiological wearables (5% of 1,128 coded product reviews described adult stress, fear or lost sleep from false alarms, preprint, not peer-reviewed); iron drops without a measured deficiency; overnight pumping to match milk; keeping her awake to hit a schedule; and rebuilding the routine in response to a two-week motor-milestone disruption. Effort has far better-evidenced targets in her waking hours.

    Plate XII

    What is unknown, and which positions will move

    The unknowns sit exactly where this family's discretionary choices sit.

    • No study has compared contact naps with cot naps in term infants on nap length, sleep architecture, night sleep or later independence, and none has tested extending a short nap.
    • No study has followed infants who sleep well at 4 months while fully parent-assisted to see how many later signal more, or whether onset method predicts it independently of temperament and feeding.
    • No randomised trial has isolated "drowsy but awake", assigned a response delay, time-matched pumped milk, manipulated light or bedtime in term infants, or randomised sleep location.
    • Melatonin suppression by evening light has never been measured under 12 months.
    • The effect of sleep training on observed attachment, emotion regulation or neurodevelopment is unknown at the 0.1–0.3 SD scale, in temperamentally reactive subgroups, for training begun at 4–6 months, and past age 6. The acute physiology of the first nights has been sampled in 4–7 infants per arm.
    • The risk of suffocation or entrapment in a deliberately prepared adult bed at 4–12 months is unquantified; bed-sharing after bottle-feeding pumped milk is unstudied; no hazard- and age-stratified case-control study of bed-sharing exists from post-2015 data.
    • No dose-response study relates objectively measured sleep at 4–12 months to later outcomes, which is why the official 12–16-hour recommendation is expert consensus anchored on normative averages; the AASM panel found "few studies" for this age band (Paruthi 2016).
    • No Mendelian-randomisation or sibling-comparison study of first-year sleep exists. Nothing in SIDS biology is usable yet: the 2022 butyrylcholinesterase finding rests on 26 cases with no threshold, sensitivity or replication (Harrington 2022).

    What would change this answer

    A problem you yourselves define after 6 months; persistent short total sleep or frequent distress waking; signs of sleep-disordered breathing or iron deficiency; or new large-sample objective data (the HBCD cohort is collecting objective first-year sleep for later outcome analysis, Pini 2024). Absent those, the right amount of additional sleep engineering for her is close to none. Unlimited time converts into advantage in two places only: keeping the safety rules at the moments constrained parents break them, and waking-hours interaction, where the measured effects are an order of magnitude larger than anything in the sleep literature.
